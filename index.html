Now

Updated November 2026

I lead the Growth Onboarding design team at HubSpot. Most of my day happens in a terminal, building things with AI.

Strategy docs, decks, research, prototypes, updates to sites like this one. A lot of it starts with pulling research and product data into a problem definition we can actually defend, and poking holes in my own thinking before anyone else has to. A first draft never leaves the session it was written in. A fresh session checks it, another one tightens it, and only then does it go to people.

I also built a small team of agents that reads the same context files I do and sends me a brief every morning: what has to happen today, what can wait until tomorrow. When it gets something wrong I tell it, and the next brief is better.

The part I care most about is the team. We share what's working in a Slack channel, and I try to post my own workflows first. We keep a shared library of Claude Code skills, and designers on the team have started writing skills of their own.

Things I believe about running a design team

Collected over ten years. Most of them learned the hard way.

  1. Show it early. A pencil sketch in crit beats a polished file nobody has seen.
  2. Nobody should be surprised by their performance review.
  3. Ask for the rationale. Good designers have one. Great ones can tell you what would change their mind.
  4. A new component has to earn its place in the system.
  5. People will tell you one thing and do another. Plan to test both.
  6. If I want the team to share how they use AI, I have to go first. Messy prompts and all.
  7. The first draft is wrong somewhere. Check it in a fresh session before you trust it, and before you ask anyone else to.
  8. The more context Claude has about the team, the org and the work, the less generic the answer. Most bad AI output is a context problem.
  9. Great work that your cross-functional partners didn't sign up for won't ship.
  10. Be ready, at any moment, to explain what your team is doing and why it matters.
  11. Bring snacks.

I grew up on Guam, and the plan was to be a musician.

Instead, here I am, more than 15 years into a career in design. Your life never turns out quite how you thought it would, does it? I've built products from scratch, built teams across a bunch of disciplines, navigated plenty of organizational drama, and shipped things that billions of people use. What I'm proudest of is the people. The wins and the failures learning moments are just punctuation on a lot of time spent with some really great humans.

The job has changed more in the last two years than in the ten before that. I still sketch and build prototypes, but the bigger shift is that I can work through a strategy, check it against real data, and argue with it before it ever reaches a meeting. It's also made me a better teacher, because the fastest way to get a team comfortable with something new is to show them your own work, mistakes included.

When I'm not working, I'm probably sketching ideas for a garden restaurant I may never open.
