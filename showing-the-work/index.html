Getting a design team from AI curiosity to AI fluency

Most knowledge workers already use AI. In early 2024, 75% did, 78% of those brought their own tools, and only 39% had been trained by their company [1]. That's old, general data, but it suggests access isn't our bottleneck. The gaps look like depth of use, trust and openness, and more licenses won't close those. My plan: measure where we are, make learning visible, share skills, test AI on the thinking as well as the making, and check what leaves the team. I drafted this with AI and had a separate session check every number against its source.

The problem

Designers lag on depth. In Figma's 2025 survey of 2,500 users, 31% of designers used AI in core work like asset generation, versus 59% of developers for code generation, and designers were less satisfied with AI tools (69% vs. 82%). Only 32% of respondents said they could rely on AI's output [2]. All of this is about making, not research or problem definition.

Trust depends on checking. In Stack Overflow's 2025 developer survey, 46% distrusted AI accuracy and 33% trusted it, and 66% were frustrated by answers "almost right, but not quite" [3]. In the 2026 survey, 48% trusted AI when they could easily validate the answers, but only 16% extended that trust to most tasks that aren't important work decisions [4].

Felt speed isn't measured speed. In METR's 2025 randomized trial, 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI but believed it had made them 20% faster [5]. METR now calls that out of date. Developers are likely faster today, it says, but its 2026 data is very weak evidence of how much, and self-reported speedups can be quite unreliable [6].

Adoption is social. In Slack's 2024 survey, 48% of desk workers were uncomfortable telling their manager they used AI for common tasks. The top reasons: it felt like cheating, or might make them look less competent or lazy. Those comfortable sharing were 67% more likely to have used it for work [7]. BCG found that positive feeling about GenAI rose from 15% to 55% of employees with strong leadership support, and that regular use was sharply higher with at least five hours of training plus access to in-person training and coaching [8]. These are correlations, and the cause may run the other way, so they make the case for a trial and no more.

My hypothesis. None of this data is about us. I expect our designers have the tools but lack shared habits, clear permission and a way to verify output, so fluency stalls at private, unchecked experiments. The baseline will confirm or change that.

What we'll do

  1. Baseline. In week one, an anonymous pulse: which tools, how often, for what, whether people would tell me, and how much they trust the output. Plus a count of prompts and skills we already share. Repeat quarterly.
  2. Learn in public. Hidden use teaches no one, so I go first: my own workflows in a team channel, failures included, and a plain statement that using AI isn't cheating. Each week I invite one person to share. Half-finished counts. No is a fine answer.
  3. Build a shared Claude Code skills library. A private prompt helps one person; a shared skill improves with every edit. Everyone gets access and setup help. It lives in version control, anyone can contribute, and each skill has an owner. First skills: "grill me" (interrogates a brief or decision), product principles, and product quality reviews.
  4. Try AI at the front of the process. This part is a bet: problem definition and synthesis are where our judgment matters most and, I suspect, where AI has been tried least. Each quarter, every designer tries AI on at least one of those steps and shows before and after in crit. "It didn't help" is a valid result. Research data goes only into approved tools, with participant identifiers removed, under research data rules I'll confirm with their owners first.
  5. Check in fresh sessions, with a person signing off. AI output is a draft until it's checked. Anything with factual claims that leaves the team gets one session that checks each claim against its fetched source, then another that critiques the argument. Because one AI session can share another's blind spots, a person makes the final call.
  6. Protect practice time. At least five hours of structured practice in month one, paired with someone further along. That mirrors BCG [8]. Since it's only a correlation, five hours is a minimum.

How we'll know

All are measured against the baseline. Given METR, self-reported speed is left out on purpose.

  • Participation: everyone shares a workflow at least once a quarter.
  • Library health: skills, contributors and revisions from version history, plus self-reported use. If only I edit it, it failed.
  • Upstream effect: share of projects using AI in problem definition or synthesis, and how many of those changed a decision, per the designer and PM at crit.
  • Verification: share of AI-assisted deliverables checked, errors caught, and errors that still escaped the team (target: near zero).
  • Comfort: whether people feel safe sharing their AI use, asked quarterly.

Risks

  • Performative sharing. The channel becomes a highlight reel. I'll keep posting failures.
  • Skill sprawl. Owners prune quarterly so the library doesn't become a junk drawer.
  • Outsourced judgment. Accepting AI's first framing could flatten our thinking. "Grill me" and critique sessions push back. I'll watch for sameness.
  • Time and cost. Licenses, practice hours and two-session checks all cost money or delivery time. If checks cost more than they catch, I'll reserve them for the highest-stakes deliverables.
  • Thin evidence. The trust and speed data comes from developers. Figma, Microsoft, Slack and BCG have a commercial stake in AI adoption, and the Microsoft and Slack data is over two years old. The baseline tells us what holds here.
  • Mandate backlash. This one is my own judgment: turn adoption into a quota and people may hide or perform their use, as they already hide what feels like cheating [7]. That's why step 4 is a trial, not a rule.
